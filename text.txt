The work conducted throughout this dissertation followed a structured progression designed to address the specific research objectives established at the outset. By mapping the development of LabReport against these goals, it is possible to demonstrate not only their fulfillment but also the broader impact of the solution on scientific research.
The project began with a phase of deep immersion into the daily activities of the \gls{inl} Nanosafety Group, which served as the primary action to satisfy Objective 1. Through a combination of interviews, brainstorming sessions, and direct workflow analysis, a comprehensive list of fifteen prioritized functionalities was identified. This collaborative process ensured that the tool was not built in a vacuum but was instead tailored to solve the real-world fragmentation of experimental data.
Building on this foundation, Objective 2 was achieved by scrutinizing the gaps in the current manual methodology, where usability and maintainability emerged as the most lacking quality attributes. This analysis allowed for the documentation of nine key non-functional requirements—exceeding the original target of three to five—which ensured the final system would be both resilient and accessible to researchers with basic informatics knowledge. Simultaneously, consultations with IT and research staff helped fulfill Objective 3 by identifying critical constraints. These included the necessity of a Python 3-based stack and the requirement for a standalone local execution model to satisfy corporate security and compliance standards.
These gathered requirements and constraints provided the architectural blueprint for Objective 4: the construction of the tool itself. The implementation of LabReport utilized a reactive, script-based architecture and the Streamlit framework to deliver a functional prototype capable of experiment tracking, automated statistical analysis, and formalized PDF report generation. By adopting a modular design, the tool successfully integrated core features such as 96-well plate data ingestion and traceability between raw measurements and final outputs.
The final phase of the dissertation addressed Objective 5 through a qualitative evaluation involving eight researchers from both academic and industrial backgrounds, surpassing the initial success criterion of five users. The feedback confirmed that the application is intuitive and aligns perfectly with established laboratory workflows. Furthermore, the successful completion of maintenance tasks by users demonstrated that the tool's architecture is indeed maintainable by the very community it serves.
Ultimately, the achievement of these individual objectives confirms the main goal of this dissertation. The results demonstrate that better tooling improves the traceability and reproducibility of experimental research without requiring disruptive changes to established practices. By transforming raw biological data into structured information and eventually into scientific knowledge, LabReport provides a robust framework that mitigates the current reproducibility crisis and streamlines the advancement of nanotoxicology.

3. Identify constraints that impact the solution space. - Review technical, organizational, and resource limitations; consult with IT and research staff. - List of constraints (e.g., compatibility, budget, time), with mitigation strategies where possible.
4. Build the tool according to the collected needs, expectations, and constraints, while following best practices. - Develop a functional prototype with core features (experiment tracking, report generation); use iterative feedback cycles. - Prototype deployed and tested; core features implemented as per requirements document.
5. Measure the success of the application with members of the Nanosafety Group to assess its functionality, overall quality, and alignment with current workflows. - Conduct user testing and surveys; compare pre- and post-implementation metrics (e.g., time savings, error reduction, user satisfaction). - Positive qualitative results from at least 5 users; documented improvements in workflow quality and user experience.
